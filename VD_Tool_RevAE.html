Basis & Assumptions — Methodology

What this tool calculates

This tool computes the voltage drop at three points — the CPP (generator) bus, an intermediate load bus if present, and the motor terminals — during the starting of a single motor on a Captive Power Plant network. All three drops are expressed in percent of base voltage. The intent is to verify that the drop stays below engineering limits (so the motor can actually accelerate) and that the generators do not trip on reduced voltage during the start.

The two methods, and when each is right

The MVA (|Z|) method uses the scalar formula ΔV = Step kVA × Z_total / Base kVA, treating the whole network as a magnitude impedance. It is fast, intuitive, and reasonable when all network elements have similar X/R ratios. In reality, source impedance is dominated by generator subtransient reactance (almost pure X), while cables add significant R. When motor starting PF is low (0.2 for DOL) and pre-load PF is high (0.85), the scalar method can be inaccurate by a few percentage points.

The R-X method uses the physically correct formula ΔV ≈ P·R + Q·X, separating real and reactive drop contributions. The source parallel is computed with complex-number arithmetic (admittance sum, then inverted). Cascaded downstream transformer stages are supported. For CPP motor starting studies this is the preferred method and the dashboard uses it alongside the MVA method for comparison.

The step load — motor only, as a phasor

During iteration, the "step kVA" that drives the drop equation is the motor's own apparent power as a phasor magnitude √(P² + Q²), not the scalar difference between total and pre-load kVA. This matters because the pre-load power factor usually differs from the motor starting PF. Earlier versions of the common Excel calculation got this wrong — a frequent pitfall rectified in this tool.

Motor behaves as constant impedance at locked rotor

At the instant of start, the motor's rotor is stationary; it draws constant-impedance current. Its real and reactive power scale with V², not V. If the voltage drops by 10%, the motor's kW and kVAR each drop by 19%. This self-limiting behaviour is why the iteration converges — if constant-power behaviour were assumed, large motors would appear to produce impossible voltage drops.

Iterative convergence

The calculation runs six iterations. In each one, the motor's demand is scaled by (1 − ΔV_previous)², the resulting kVA is dropped through the network impedance, and a new ΔV is computed. The sequence converges in four to five iterations for well-behaved cases. For extreme cases (motor far too large for the network), the iteration oscillates, which is detected by the Design Capacity Finder's convergence guard and treated as "non-startable" rather than returning a bogus fixed point.

Sources — subtransient reactance only

Generator sources are modelled with their subtransient reactance X″d alone, representing the worst-case voltage dip in the first few cycles of the start. The transient (X′d) and steady-state (Xs) stages are not modelled; actual voltage recovery after AVR response will show less sustained drop. This conservative choice is standard for motor starting verification.

Downstream network

The path from CPP bus to motor terminals is modelled as a cascade of up to seven stages, each selectable as a cable or a transformer. Any stage can be flagged "load bus" so the tool reports the drop at that intermediate point. Cable impedances convert from Ω/km × length / runs to per-unit on the base impedance at the stage's own bus voltage. Transformer impedances use the standard conversion Z_sys = Z_self × (MVA_base / MVA_rated) × (kV_rated / kV_base)², with the voltage ratio correction that is commonly missed.

Torque adequacy

During a reduced-voltage start, the motor's accelerating torque scales approximately with V². Industry practice sets a minimum voltage of around 0.80 p.u. at motor terminals so that developed torque exceeds load torque throughout the acceleration curve. The tool checks V_motor = 1 − ΔV_motor against this threshold and flags cases where it falls short.

Pre-load during start

The pre-load at CPP bus is assumed to behave as constant-impedance during the voltage dip — a reasonable approximation for industrial loads dominated by motors. Its kW and kVAR are not iterated explicitly; its effect on steady-state voltage before the start is assumed to leave V ≈ 1.0 p.u. under AVR regulation.

N-1 contingency

The tool simulates the loss of each currently-running source in turn and reports the voltage drops that would result. If any contingency breaches a limit, a warning banner appears recommending that motor starting be sequenced after confirming all sources are available. This is often the binding operational constraint even when the base case is comfortable.

Standards and limits

The tool ships with three preset limit sets. IEC 60909: 10%/10%/20% at CPP/Load/Motor plus V ≥ 0.80 p.u. IEEE 141 Red Book: 5%/7%/15% plus V ≥ 0.85 p.u. NEMA MG-1: 8%/10%/20% plus V ≥ 0.80 p.u. All four values are user-editable to accommodate project-specific requirements or company standards. The warning (amber) threshold is automatically computed at 80% of the fail limit.

Cable R and X — from standard tables

In Basic mode the user does not enter cable R and X directly. Instead the user selects Cable Type (XLPE, PVC or EPR), Cores (3-core or single-cores in trefoil), Conductor Material (Copper or Aluminium), Cross-section from standard sizes (25 to 630 mm²), Length and Number of runs. The tool looks up base resistance and reactance from a built-in library based on IEC 60502-1 for XLPE-insulated three-core cables at 90 °C conductor temperature. Correction factors are then applied for the chosen type and cores: PVC-insulated cables see R multiplied by 0.85 (lower conductor temperature at 70 °C), EPR identical to XLPE at 90 °C, and single-cores in trefoil see X multiplied by 1.15 because the geometric mean distance between phase conductors is larger than for a packaged three-core cable. Typical values at common sizes: Cu 240 mm² = 0.098 Ω/km R, 0.072 Ω/km X at XLPE 90 °C; Al 240 mm² = 0.161 Ω/km R, 0.072 Ω/km X. The computed R and X are shown as an auto-note below each cable card so the engineer sees exactly what values the calculation uses. In Advanced mode the user retains direct R and X entry via the Downstream Stages card for cables outside the standard library (choose Custom as material).

Transformer X/R ratio — typical values and manual override

In Basic mode the user does not enter R% directly. Instead the user enters only impedance Z% and the tool derives R% and X% from Z and a typical X/R ratio by rating per IS 2026 / IEC 60076: under 0.5 MVA → X/R = 2.5; 0.5 to 2.5 MVA → 5; 2.5 to 10 MVA → 8; 10 to 50 MVA → 12; above 50 MVA → 18. From Z% and X/R, the tool back-solves R% = Z%/√(1+(X/R)²) and X% = (X/R)·R%. The X/R, R% and X% actually used are shown in the auto-note. A user who has a specific test certificate or manufacturer data can switch the X/R source to Manual and enter the actual value; the tool then uses that for the decomposition instead of the typical.

Permissible voltage drop limits — presets and manual override

In Basic mode the default limits are IEC 60909: 10% at CPP bus, 10% at Load bus, 20% at motor terminals, and minimum Vmot ≥ 0.80 p.u. for torque adequacy. For projects that follow IEEE 141 (Red Book) the typical limits are tighter at 5% / 7% / 15% and Vmot ≥ 0.85 p.u. For NEMA MG-1, 8% / 10% / 20% and Vmot ≥ 0.80 p.u. The user can switch the Permissible Voltage Drops source to Manual in Card 7 and enter any set of four values appropriate to the project standard or client specification. In Advanced mode all limits are user-editable through the Permissible Limits sidebar card.

Why MVA and R-X methods give different results

The MVA method is a quick first-pass approximation: it treats all source and path impedances as pure reactance (ignoring resistance) and computes the voltage drop as roughly ΔV ≈ step-kVA / short-circuit-MVA at the bus. This works well for stiff grids with mostly inductive impedance (large transformers, short cables) where resistance is a small fraction of total impedance. The R-X method uses proper complex phasor arithmetic — each impedance is a complex number Z = R + jX, each current is a complex phasor, and the voltage drop is ΔV = Re[I* × Z], the projection of the voltage phasor onto the pre-start operating-point voltage. This correctly accounts for the resistance in long cables and for the phase relationship between current and impedance.

The two methods converge for networks dominated by large transformers (high X/R ratios, say above 10). They diverge whenever cables carry a significant fraction of the total impedance — long low-voltage cables, aluminium conductors, or large cross-sections where R / X approaches 1 — because the MVA method's assumption of pure reactance breaks down. For a motor on a long cable supplied by a modest source, the R-X method typically shows a larger voltage drop than MVA by 10 % to 30 %, because resistance adds to total impedance magnitude on top of reactance. The R-X number is physically correct; the MVA number is a conservative short-hand that becomes optimistic (under-reports drop) as cables dominate. The tool defaults to R-X Method; switch to MVA Method only when you need a quick sanity check against hand calculations or legacy reports that used the MVA shortcut.

Per-unit voltage correction — multi-voltage topologies

From Rev S the engine no longer applies the extraneous (kV_rated / kV_base)² correction to generator and transformer impedances. In single-voltage downstream networks (where the Load bus matches the CPP bus voltage) this correction was always ≈ 1 and had no practical effect. In multi-voltage networks (for example a 34.5 kV CPP stepping down through a Load transformer to an 11 kV Load bus) the correction was spuriously small, dividing transformer impedance by the voltage ratio squared and under-reporting voltage drops by nearly an order of magnitude. The corrected formula Z_pu(system) = Z%_self × (MVA_base / MVA_rated) is physically correct across voltage levels because transformer turn ratios cancel voltage squared terms in a properly chosen per-unit system.

What this tool does NOT model

Dynamic motor speed-time behaviour (acceleration curve, run-up time), AVR response and voltage recovery after start, protection relay operation during the dip, harmonic effects of soft-starters or VFDs on waveform quality, transformer tap position effects, and generator governor response are all out of scope. For those, a dedicated time-domain simulator (ETAP, DIgSILENT, PSS/E) is appropriate.

Verification against the rectified Excel

For a reference case of a 3000 kW motor at 11 kV with three 48 MVA generators running and 84 MW pre-load (DOL start, IEC limits), the tool returns 5.01% at CPP bus, 9.04% at load bus, 10.33% at motor terminal, with V_motor = 0.897 p.u. — matching the hand-calculated phasor-method result within rounding. Common Excel calculations differ because of three recurring errors: scalar step load (should be phasor), linear V scaling (should be V²), and missing (kV_rated/kV_base)² ratio in per-unit conversion. All three are handled correctly here.

Dynamic motor speed-time behaviour (acceleration curve, run-up time), AVR response and voltage recovery after start, protection relay operation during the dip, harmonic effects of soft-starters or VFDs on waveform quality, transformer tap position effects, and generator governor response are all out of scope. For those, a dedicated time-domain simulator (ETAP, DIgSILENT, PSS/E) is appropriate.

Verification against the rectified Excel

For a reference case of a 3000 kW motor at 11 kV with three 48 MVA generators running and 84 MW pre-load (DOL start, IEC limits), the tool returns 5.01% at CPP bus, 9.04% at load bus, 10.33% at motor terminal, with V_motor = 0.897 p.u. — matching the hand-calculated phasor-method result within rounding. Common Excel calculations differ because of three recurring errors: scalar step load (should be phasor), linear V scaling (should be V²), and missing (kV_rated/kV_base)² ratio in per-unit conversion. All three are handled correctly here.
